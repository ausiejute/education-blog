# 🧠 Mindset

A blog post on how to deal with getting stuck.

## Tips ##

- *Reframe "Stuck" as "Paused"—Not "Broken"*.
There's a psychological term called "fixed mindset" versus "growth mindset." When you think "I'm stuck because I'm not good at this," your brain interprets it as evidence of permanent incompetence. Your nervous system actually tightens—literally. Your prefrontal cortex (where problem-solving happens) gets less blood flow. But when you reframe it as "I'm paused, and pausing is part of the process," something shifts neurologically. You move from threat mode to learning mode. What this looks like: Next time you're stuck, say it out loud differently. Instead of "I can't figure this out," try "I haven't figured this out *yet*." That one word—yet—signals to your brain that this is temporary. It's the difference between a fixed state and an active process. You've probably solved hundreds of problems in your life; this is just the next one in the queue.

- *Step Away—But With Intention.*
Neurologically, your brain doesn't actually work better when you're white-knuckling a problem. The default mode network in your brain (the part that makes creative connections) activates when you're not focusing intensely. This is why the best ideas hit you in the shower. But here's where psychology meets execution: stepping away is only effective if you've done the groundwork first. What this looks like: Before you take a break, write down exactly where you are—the error message, the line of code, what you've tried. This external dump does two things: it prevents your brain from spinning on the problem (which wastes mental energy), and it primes your subconscious to work on it while you're away. Then actually step away—take a walk, drink water, talk to someone. Give your brain 15-30 minutes to cycle through the problem without your conscious interference. Often, you'll return with fresh perspective.

- *Debug Your Assumptions, Not Just Your Code*.
This one comes straight from coaching executives through their sticking points: sometimes you're stuck not because the answer is hard, but because you're asking the wrong question. In coding, this happens constantly. You're debugging Line 47 when the real problem is your understanding of how the function should work in the first place. What this looks like: When you've been stuck for more than 10 minutes, stop coding. Instead, write out in plain English: "I expect X to happen, but Y is happening instead. This tells me that [my assumption about how this works] might be wrong." Often, the moment you articulate your assumption, you'll spot the flaw. You're not dumb—you're just operating on incomplete information. That's fixable. In fact, that's learning.
